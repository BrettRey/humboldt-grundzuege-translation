## claude (claude-fable-5-2)

I therefore begin with the general presentation of the structure of the American languages, in such a way that I shall attempt to state completely, along all the lines on which languages are to be judged, the peculiarities of the American languages in general and the differences of the individual languages among themselves.

In this way the presentation of the structure of the individual languages is, in its principal parts, woven into the general presentation, and only the less essential, and that which constitutes the true individuality of the individual language, the determinate designation by sounds, falls away.

On the other hand, this procedure does admittedly tear apart the portrayal of the individual languages and make a complete overview of the structure of each one impossible.

Yet one also gains the advantage of seeing every peculiarity of every individual language, within the general picture, right at the place where it can be compared with those of the other languages and appraised.

Anyone who is accustomed to investigating the structure of many languages will, however, hardly be able to rate precisely this advantage too highly.

For it is one of the most distressing experiences, though no less common for that, that when one has with toil and exertion thought one's way into the structure of a language, one nevertheless remains undecided as to how one is now to place this language within the domain of the whole study of language, and how, amid the diversity of the merits and defects that are to be weighed against one another, one is to determine its worth.

The overview that we just now found wanting in this procedure can nevertheless be regained to some degree by means of a collating index.
