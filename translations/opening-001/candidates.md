## Claude — claude-fable-5-2

If it perhaps seems strange to make precisely the languages of one part of the world the object of an inquiry, this view, at first sight merely geographical, is justified in the case of America by the separate situation of this part of the world and the particular character of its languages. In Europe, Asia and Africa languages so different have flowed together that the study of languages, at least, can find no occasion to investigate them precisely in the connection into which, always more or less by chance, they have as it happens entered within the boundaries of the same part of the world. Moreover, the language stocks of each of these show the most considerable differences, both in their structure and in the stages of development they have attained. America, by contrast, apart from the most recent European immigrations, which have touched the inner national individuality of this part of the world only through their destructive influence, has undergone either none at all or only such as have left scarcely a trace that could be called historical. Whatever, therefore, the origin of the American peoples and languages may be, their fortunes have for many centuries been shaped only through themselves and through contact with one another. The multitude, too vast to survey, of tribes, hordes and families from the farthest north to the farthest south of this extensive part of the world have, for as long as we know its history and down to the European subjugation, free from foreign influence, come into a ferment of their powers only through themselves, and have imparted to one another only their own individualities. Whatever foreign matter may therefore have been brought in at a still earlier time has afterwards, for centuries, undergone this abrading influence of an indigenous intercourse confined to itself.
