## Introduction

**1** If it may seem strange to make precisely the languages of a single continent the subject of an inquiry, this approach, at first sight merely geographical, is justified in the case of America by the continent’s isolated position and the distinctive character of its languages. In Europe, Asia, and Africa, such diverse languages have come together that the study of language, at least, can find no reason to investigate them in the particular grouping in which, always more or less by chance, they now find themselves within the boundaries of the same continent. The language stocks of each of these continents also show very considerable differences, both in their structure and in the stages of development they have attained. America, by contrast, apart from the most recent European immigration, which has affected the continent’s inner national distinctiveness only through its destructive influence, has experienced either no immigration at all or only immigration of which scarcely a trace that could be called historical remains. Whatever the origin of the American peoples and languages may therefore be, their fortunes have for many centuries been shaped solely from within and through contact with one another. Throughout the period for which we know the history of this vast continent, down to subjugation by Europeans[^subjugation-scope], its countless tribes, hordes, and families, from the farthest north to the farthest south, came, free from foreign influence, into a ferment of their powers through themselves alone. They imparted only their own distinctive traits to one another.[^american-contact] Anything foreign that may have been introduced at a still earlier time has therefore subsequently undergone, for centuries, this wearing-down influence of self-contained native interaction.

**2** Further support for this comes from the striking similarity of the American languages. There have certainly been substantial differences in the development of the nations to which they belong, differences unmistakable in the languages themselves as well[^nations-or-languages]; yet the lack of alphabetic writing, and hence of literature in the proper sense, places them all, without exception, on an equal footing at a lower level.[^writing-and-literature] It is true that the sometimes wrongly asserted sameness of their structure disappears under more penetrating investigation, as does their deviation from the structure of all other languages, which has also been asserted. Nevertheless, all the languages of America do in fact exhibit broad features of similarity, and the north and south of the continent do not permit genuinely separating boundaries to be drawn even in this respect.

**3** It may therefore well be said, with good reason, that the general study of language calls for considering the American languages in their interconnection, inquiring into and describing their structure, and comparing it with that of the languages of the other continents. My intention in the present work is to do this to the best of my abilities, with due completeness, but above all through inquiry that penetrates as deeply as possible into the essential character of these languages. To achieve this aim, I have for a number of years not merely studied the individual languages but also prepared my own treatments of them, and for an even longer time collected materials, chiefly in manuscript. Collecting of this kind can never attain completeness; that is inherent in the nature of the matter itself. But the resources that are at my disposal and are to be regarded as sources for this investigation provide, when combined with what has already been very usefully prepared in several other works, abundant material for a treatment covering the whole field of this investigation, and sufficient material for a reliable assessment.

**4** For a time I wavered between two plans: publishing the individual grammars and dictionaries first, or first presenting the general conclusions drawn from the research into the individual languages. The latter course has the disadvantage that the reader can check the correctness of the general claims only very incompletely: resources for studying the individual languages must first be created for the reader, given the rarity and often very inconvenient arrangement of those that exist. But I was deterred from the former course by the protracted nature of the undertaking and the length of time that, given the difficulty of publishing such works, would necessarily have elapsed in the process.

**5** I therefore begin with the general presentation of the structure of the American languages, in such a way that I shall try to set out fully, in every respect in which languages are to be assessed, the distinctive characteristics of the American languages in general and the differences among the individual languages. In this way, the most important parts of the presentation of the structure of the individual languages[^parts-of-presentation] are woven into the general presentation; only the less essential matters and what constitutes the true individuality of the individual language—the specific designation by sounds—are left out.[^sound-designation] On the other hand, this procedure admittedly breaks up the description of the individual languages and makes a complete overview of the structure of each one impossible. Yet one also gains the advantage of seeing every distinctive characteristic of each individual language within the general picture, at the very point where it can be compared with those of the other languages and appraised. Anyone accustomed to investigating the structure of many languages will, however, scarcely be able to rate this very advantage too highly. For it is one of the most disheartening experiences, though no less common for that, to have worked one’s way with effort and exertion into an understanding of a language’s structure and yet remain undecided about how that language is now to be placed within the field of language study as a whole, and how its worth is to be determined amid the variety of merits and shortcomings to be weighed against one another. The overview we have just found lacking in this procedure can nevertheless be recovered to some extent through an index that brings the material together.

**6** It may, however, also be left for later to follow the general presentation of the American languages with grammars and dictionaries of the individual languages; this could be done all the more briefly after the general part, since that part permits one merely to refer briefly to particulars already contained in it. I shall take care, however, to weave a great many particulars into the general part, so that no excessively large gap arises even if I should be unable to make available the separate treatments of the various languages.[^american-publication] For these treatments, by their nature, must take time and involve several people undertaking such work for the same purpose and from the same points of view. If the general study of language is ever to become a study accessible also to those for whom it is not an exclusive vocation, there must be grammars and dictionaries of all known languages prepared according to the same principles, and these, as a continuing body of material for new research, must be corrected and expanded in keeping with ever-advancing knowledge of languages.

**7** The languages of a region can be presented chiefly in two ways that differ in purpose and treatment: first in themselves, as idioms[^idiom] subsumed under human language in general, as species under a genus; second in relation to the place and the people to which they belong, in their geographical and historical connections. This is no different from what one does with the products of nature, with plants and kinds of stone. One characterizes, identifies, and names the individual ones, assigning them their position in the system without regard to the place they occupy; but one also describes the entire vegetation cover and all the rock deposits of a region. In both cases, the one way actually presents the languages and the natural objects, while the other presents not so much these themselves as the region in terms of them. The general study of language as a whole must be pursued in these two directions; so far, however, the latter, geographical and historical direction has been the more prevalent. These two perspectives cannot, however, be completely separated, since every language, even when regarded merely as a new genus[^genus-shift], must nevertheless be investigated and examined together with all the circumstances that influence it, and hence in its local and historical constitution, and since describing a region in terms of its languages presupposes knowledge of their distinctive character. Yet these two modes of presentation nevertheless turn out to be entirely different in content and method, and all investigations into the descent and relatedness of languages, for example, belong primarily to the latter.

I begin with the presentation of the languages in themselves, since knowledge of these must always come first, and this treatment is also by far the more important for the study of language. Accordingly, when referring to individual American languages, I shall always bring them forward in the order appropriate to the subject under discussion on each occasion. Where that purpose leaves the order a matter of indifference, however, I shall follow the geographical order, proceeding, in accordance with the configuration of America, from south to north and from west to east, so as to keep the two ways of presenting languages distinguished above connected, by this and by every other means that does not interfere with the main purpose.

## I. Presentation of the American languages in themselves and in their distinctive character

**8** There is as yet no proven procedure for achieving the aim I set myself here, no attempt successful to any degree at portraying the distinctive character of a language in such a way that its relation to others and its place in the domain of languages in general would emerge much as a plant genus acquires its fixed position in the system through the botanical determination of its characters. People have indeed hit upon the idea of classifying languages by the similarities and differences of their grammatical forms, according to whether they possess articles, gender marking, a dual, separable or inseparable pronouns, inflected verbs or verbs combined with auxiliary words, and so forth. Groupings of this kind may afford various subordinate benefits, but even a little reflection shows that they can never lead to a classification decisive as to the essential nature of languages. The features taken here as the basis of division are merely the technical means of languages, whose use or absence proves little about their inner nature and almost nothing about their agreement or disagreement as wholes. English and Danish are certainly very closely related to German, and yet the former lacks gender marking, while the latter has a passive formed by inflection.[^english-gender] What advantage, then, could there be in tearing these languages away from our own in a classification of languages and placing them with those with which they share these properties? These technical means also change, arise and disappear; one takes the place of another, and the absence of a particular form does not mean the absence of the effect it otherwise produces in the language. Often it is not even easy to determine whether or not one of these forms is present in a language. Examples abound for every point touched on here. An inflected passive was not always foreign to German; the dual disappeared in the later common Greek dialect; Coptic quite ordinarily uses the inflected verb in place of the participle, yet the mode of representation is nonetheless that of the participle[^coptic-participle-comparison]; in some languages it is difficult to say whether or not they have an article, as is already shown by the transition from the Indic demonstrative pronoun[^greek-article-development] to the Homeric article, and from this to the article used in later Greek.[^historical-german-passive]

**9** In general, one must take care not to carry the comparison of the system of languages[^system-of-languages] with systems of nature further than the subject permits. A language cannot be dissected like a natural body; even in the mass of words and rules it presents, it is not material simply lying there, but an activity, a mental process, just as life is a bodily process. Nothing relating to it can be compared with anatomical treatment, but only with physiological treatment; nothing in it is static, everything is dynamic. Even dead languages are no exception in this respect. What one investigates in them is the thought of the past that they preserve, and thought is always the breath of something living, always capable of being confined within a fixed form only in such a way that this very confinement secures its natural boundlessness, its freedom to pass into other and yet other forms.[^thought-other-forms] For it is the essence of language to draw a boundary in such a way that the determination contained in it cancels, of itself, the restriction possible within that boundary, as every word and every form, by virtue of their formation, call forth others standing beside, above and below them; language unceasingly articulates parts and wholes into one another in such a way that parts can become wholes and wholes parts.

**10** Language must therefore be considered in its living operation if one wishes truly to investigate its nature and arrive at a classification of several languages. Yet precisely because it is a mental process, neither task can be accomplished with the same success as in the case of physical substances. One cannot avoid also dissecting it, as a fixed and finished body, into its constituents; but this task must always remain subordinate to the higher consideration: through what original cast of mind and manner of sound, and by what technical means, does each language achieve what individually modified realization of the general purpose of language?

**11** Thus, according to what has been said here, two paths present themselves for gaining complete insight into the structure of a language, and they must be followed one after the other. On one, one examines the nature of the grammatical and lexical means the language employs: declension, conjugation, compounding, derivation, and so forth. On the other, one investigates the manner in which these means are applied and used, the overall treatment they receive, and explores, from every side and as completely as possible, the language’s procedure as a whole. True insight into the language’s essence depends on first presenting that procedure. Several of the points that come into consideration here can, to be sure, readily be given as examples. For example, one asks: does the language keep these means apart from one another in fixed forms, or throw them together and confuse them? Does it mark them in a simple, regular manner, or in one that is contingently uneven and divergent? Does it attend with sensitivity to the mutual influence of neighbouring sounds and subject it to fixed rules? In what sequence, in constructing a period[^period], does it bring the parts of the total concept before the mind’s view? In its words, does it start from basic sounds[^basic-sounds] formed simply or in manifold ways? In the modification, compounding and placement of words, does it allow usage freedom or confine it within fixed rules? And so forth. Yet a complete and systematic enumeration of these manifold questions must rest on a deeper investigation, since precisely this kind of analysis of language has hitherto been pursued less extensively.

**12** To name briefly the distinction suggested here, one may call the one the investigation of language’s procedure, the other the investigation of its constituents, provided one does not forget that this opposition must not be taken too strictly. The former is also more a consideration of language as a whole, the latter a consideration of it in detail.

**13** I begin with the former, and do not believe that I am thereby following an unnatural method in letting the particular follow the general here, as I have already done in the divisions made so far (see 7). Study requires a different path from the presentation of what has been learned through study. In devoting oneself to study, one must learn the individual American languages before reflecting on them all, and in each ascend step by step from the sound element to the formation of the sentence. But the picture that one can draw, after this work, of the individual languages and of them all becomes more vivid and impresses itself better upon the soul if one begins by indicating its outermost outlines and gradually defines it more closely, bringing out its individual features. What is characteristic of languages does indeed reside in their individual constituents, but in such small particles, with such pale colours (if these expressions may be permitted), that one seldom recognizes it clearly enough in those constituents taken separately. Once the character as a whole has been drawn, however, it becomes much easier to see from it how it requires this or that constitution of the constituents, or rather arises from that constitution.

**14** But there is also a deeper reason in the nature of language that justifies the choice of this order. Despite the similarity of the causes that produce all languages, of their technical means and of their purpose, every language possesses a distinct individuality[^individuality-across-passages], which is fully felt only in its working as a whole.[^joint-working] The analysis into parts necessary to transform this feeling into knowledge always somewhat obscures the apprehension of living individuality, if only because this very transformation of feeling into knowledge can never be wholly complete. If one begins with the total impression, however, that feeling at least extends over the whole course of the investigation that follows. If one reverses the order, or even stops at analysis, one obtains a long series of analyses of languages made according to a single plan (since the different plans better suited to each individuality could arise only from knowledge of that individuality), without recognizing or feeling the essential distinctive character of a single one of them. One learns a great deal about them, but not the one thing that matters. The more detailed the analyses, the more they disperse the impression of the whole. Merely comparing samples of language, even meagre ones not altogether suitably chosen, is far better suited to grasping the total impression of a language’s character. I by no means deny, however, that if one wishes to make this comparison fruitful and demonstrate the character of the language in its constituents, this path presents obstacles that are difficult, and in part altogether impossible, to remove. It is therefore all the more important at least to take, from the outset, a direction that facilitates success.

**15** Incidentally, the arrangement chosen here will also have the advantage of preventing the fragmentation, feared above (4), of what is to be said about each individual American language, and of giving instead a total picture of them all at the very outset. For a portrayal of a language in its operation can be drawn only with a certain unity, and even the points that must also be explored individually here always stand in a closer relation to the whole. The grammatical elements (articles, case markers, the verb, and so forth), by contrast, constitute parts whose connection with the character of the whole is not always equally visible, and their portrayal can be carried out separately in as many monographs, each extending across all the American languages.

## Separate later pilot: passage 147

This later passage is included as a separate test of the translation method. It does not follow passage 15 in the source. Its opening refers back to the preceding argument, which is outside this selection.

**147** Here, however, one circumstance must not be overlooked. It does indeed lessen the disadvantage arising from the lack of genuine grammatical forms in languages, and could be regarded as a decisive objection to what has just been said. Even if a grammatical form has no designation in the language, it is nevertheless present, as a guiding law of understanding, in those who speak the language. At the same time, it is also indicated in the language in some way, however that may be. It may therefore even seem doubtful whether it should be attributed to the language or denied to it, since, after all, by language we understand not merely the dead sounds but also the speakers’ apprehension of them. Yet it remains no less necessary to distinguish this apprehension precisely from the dead sounds, and here to speak only of what actually lies in the sound perceptible to the senses. By grammatical form, therefore, I here understand an indication of the grammatical relation in the audible part of language itself that is so fixed and pure that the grammatical relation indicated is definitely recognized in it, and that the manner of indication cannot be taken as a designation of a concept at all but immediately, through itself, makes manifest the distinction between the designation of concepts and the designation of relations. Given the order deliberately chosen (134.), I can explain the manner of indication only later. Here I do not even exclude indication through word order, provided that it is absolutely fixed, immovable, and refers to a fixed point that is not itself recognizable merely by its position. If I now assume a case in which such an indication of a grammatical form as that just described is entirely absent from a language, or is effected not formally but through a concept with substantive content[^substantive-content], the grammatical form[^form-or-indication] is nevertheless brought into the language, and thus in turn also acts back from the language upon the mind. Thus, for example, in Chinese there is no designation of the verb as a grammatical form.[^chinese-grammatical-form] But the language possesses words which, although grammatically they could also be nouns, are always taken only as verbs in the language. The Chinese speaker therefore never sees anything other than a verb in such a word, and for him the language consequently has verbs, provided one pays no attention to whether their verbal nature lies in a grammatical designation or in habitual linguistic usage. The same is found where a word in Chinese can be used equally well as a noun or as a verb, but the context of meaning or position indicates that the latter use is intended. Yet position in Chinese is not an indication of grammatical form in the sense just established, if only because it lacks a fixed centre outside itself. It also happens in languages that words originally possessing substantive content pass into actual relational words. In Chinese, ì[^historical-chinese-transcription] is a verb and means to use, to employ. But it also serves as a preposition, and Mr Abel-Rémusat[^remusat-citation][^editor-remusat-page][^citation-number-context] very rightly observes that the nation, frequently using the word in this latter meaning, gradually forgets its original verbal sense entirely. In this instance, admittedly, such forgetting is more difficult, since the word is still current as a verb even today. But very often the situation is such that the original substantive meaning has disappeared entirely and can scarcely still be discerned etymologically; in that case, such a grammatical word functions solely as a relational concept. This, however, can only have happened little by little, through a gradual transition. Consequently, it is no longer always possible to tell from the language alone what is taken as truly grammatical, as a genuine and pure designation of relation; for that, one would have to know the nation’s manner of apprehension, and to know it at different epochs.

## Terminology guide

These are local reading aids. The [full termbase](../../docs/preparation/termbase.md) retains contextual choices, alternatives and source attestations.

**Distinctive character, character, individuality** (*Eigenthümlichkeit*, *Charakter*, *Individualität*). These expressions can overlap. The present passages do not establish an invariant three-way technical division; passages 5 and 14 place different emphases on Individualität.

**Procedure and constituents** (*Verfahren*, *Bestandtheile*). Procedure concerns how a language employs and coordinates its means; constituents are the grammatical and lexical components. Passage 12 expressly makes the opposition non-rigid.

**Activity and operation** (*Verrichtung*, *Wirken*, *Zusammenwirken*). Activity in 9 renders Verrichtung in contrast to a stock of material; operation or working renders Wirken. Their joint-working sense is important, but no fixed philosophical opposition between these English words is asserted.

**Boundary, determination, restriction** (*Begränzung*, *Bestimmung*, *Beschränkung*). Passage 9 uses three distinct words: making a boundary, the determination it contains, and the restriction thereby made possible. Cancels renders aufhebt without fixing a later philosophical doctrine.

**Mind and soul** (*Geist*, *Seele*). The inherited English uses soul for Seele in 13 and mind for it in 147; that English variation does not establish a German lexical contrast. Geist and Seele are not assigned an invariant division into separate faculties here. The main text is preserved in this annotation pass.

**Cast of mind and manner of sound** (*Geistes- und Tonart*). The compressed phrase in 10 concerns mental and sonic character. Manner of sound leaves open its historical range; it does not designate a musical key or a modern tone-language class.

**Apprehension and knowledge** (*Anschauung*, *Auffassung*, *Erkenntniss*). The distinctions are local: 14 contrasts apprehended living individuality with knowledge obtained by analysis; 147 contrasts speakers’ apprehension with what is audibly marked. No uniform English substitution settles their philosophical relations.

**Substantive and relational** (*Sachbegriff*, *Sachwörter*, *Sachbedeutung*, *Verhältnisswörter*, *Verhältnissbegriff*). The contrast in 147 is between what a word means in its own right and a grammatical relation it expresses. The meaning “to use, to employ” is an example of content meaning; in a later stage of a word’s history, such an earlier meaning may be lost while a relational use remains. “Substantive” does not mean “noun,” and “relational word” is not imposed as a universal modern word-class label.

**Noun and verb** (*Nomen*, *Nomina*, *Verbum*). Noun is the working English label for the Latin Nomen in 147; its precise historical extension remains unsettled. The examples describe a word’s use as a noun or a verb, without establishing a modern category-membership test.

## Notes

[^source-manuscript]: [Summary of the 1906 editor’s note.] The editor reports a manuscript of 148 half-written folio pages in the Royal Library in Berlin and notes Steinthal’s use of selected passages in his edition of Humboldt’s language-philosophical works. (1906 Ed.)

[^subjugation-scope]: “Down to subjugation by Europeans” adopts the temporal reading of German bis auf. “Except for subjugation by Europeans” remains possible; that reading would make subjugation an exception to the claim of freedom from foreign influence. (Trans.)

[^american-contact]: The Norse settlement at L’Anse aux Meadows, dating from around 1000 and discovered in 1960, supplies archaeological evidence of pre-Columbian European presence. This qualifies the picture of isolation; it does not by itself establish an influence on American languages. See [Parks Canada](https://www.parcs.canada.ca/lhn-nhs/nl/meadows/culture). (Trans.)

[^nations-or-languages]: “In the languages themselves” interprets a German pronoun that can also refer to the nations. The later “them all” likewise leaves open whether nations or languages are placed on the asserted scale. (Trans.)

[^writing-and-literature]: Alphabetic writing is only one kind of writing. Maya script combines word signs and syllable signs; absence of an alphabet therefore does not establish absence of writing. “Literature in the proper sense” and the scale of a “lower level” express Humboldt’s evaluative criterion. See [SLUB Dresden](https://www.slub-dresden.de/en/explore/manuscripts/the-dresden-maya-codex/deciphering). (Trans.)

[^parts-of-presentation]: “The most important parts” is taken to mean parts of the presentation. The German possessive also permits parts of the individual languages; its form does not settle the reference. (Trans.)

[^sound-designation]: The German Lautbezeichnung can concern designation through sounds or the specification of those sounds. The present rendering follows the former reading; the latter remains possible. (Trans.)

[^american-publication]: Humboldt’s larger American project remained unfinished, although he left substantial grammatical studies, including a Nahuatl grammar (modern manuscript-based edition, 1994). The Nahuatl dictionary, published in 2000, was conceived by Humboldt but compiled and revised chiefly by Eduard Buschmann, who continued the work after Humboldt’s death. See [Drechsel 2024, chapters 7–8](https://doi.org/10.1017/9781108966801) and [Trabant 2002, §4.2](https://shesl.org/trabant-legs-intempestif/). (Trans.)

[^idiom]: “Idioms” here means individual languages or varieties, rather than idiomatic expressions. (Trans.)

[^genus-shift]: The earlier analogy makes individual languages species under the genus of human language. Here each language is itself called a “new genus”: the German repeats Gattung. This apparent shift in classificatory level is retained; “new kind” is another possible reading. (Trans.)

[^english-gender]: English distinguishes he, she and it, although nouns are not commonly marked for gender. Humboldt’s claim is therefore too broad if read as excluding every gender distinction; a restriction to nominal gender is possible but is not explicit here. See [English Grammar Today](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/grammar/british-grammar/gender). (Trans.)

[^coptic-participle-comparison]: Coptic circumstantial clauses can do work rendered by participles in Greek or English: for example, “He found them sleeping” (Matthew 26:40). See [Plumley, §§197–197a](https://cdn.suscopts.org/files/deacons/coptic/introductory-sahidic-coptic-grammar_john-martin-plumley-1948.pdf); this supports the functional comparison without identifying Humboldt’s precise intended construction. (Trans.)

[^greek-article-development]: Greek article forms retain demonstrative uses in Homer alongside emerging article uses; see [Smyth, §§1099–1102](https://grammars.alpheios.net/smyth/xhtml/body.1_div1.4_div2.16.html). The comparison here does not establish a direct derivation of Greek from an Indian language. (Trans.)

[^historical-german-passive]: Inflected passive forms are attested in Gothic; Old High German has passive constructions with auxiliaries. “German” is retained here; a broader historical use for Germanic may be intended. See [Lehmann, §5.5](https://lrc.la.utexas.edu/books/pgmc/5-syntax). (Trans.)

[^system-of-languages]: “System of languages” takes the German Sprachsystem in the classificatory context of passage 8. “System of language,” referring to language’s internal organization, is another possible reading. (Trans.)

[^thought-other-forms]: The German leaves the noun unexpressed. “Forms” is supplied from the preceding “fixed form”; “thoughts” is also possible. (Trans.)

[^period]: A “period” here is an extended, articulated sentence, rather than a full stop. Periodenbau concerns its construction or structure. (Trans.)

[^basic-sounds]: “Basic sounds” (Grundlaute) leaves open whether elementary speech sounds or root-like sound formations are in view; it is not a substitution of the modern term phonemes. (Trans.)

[^individuality-across-passages]: In passage 5, “true individuality” was tied to specific designation by sounds; here individuality is felt in the language’s working as a whole. The German uses Individualität in both places. These different emphases are retained without imposing a fixed opposition between individuality, distinctive character and character. (Trans.)

[^joint-working]: “Its” takes the language as the referent. The German also permits “their working together,” referring to the causes, technical means and purpose just listed. (Trans.)

[^substantive-content]: “Substantive” means the content meaning of a word, as distinct from the grammatical relation it marks. For example, “to use, to employ” is the content meaning of the Chinese word also used as a preposition. An “original substantive meaning” is an earlier content meaning that may later be lost; “substantive” does not mean “noun.” (Trans.)

[^form-or-indication]: “The grammatical form” supplies the referent of German sie. “The indication” is also grammatically possible; the surrounding discussion favours the form as the chosen reading. (Trans.)

[^chinese-grammatical-form]: “Grammatical form” has the restricted sense defined above. The argument distinguishes a grammatical relation from its formal designation; it explicitly allows Chinese verbs to be recognized through habitual use in the following sentences. (Trans.)

[^historical-chinese-transcription]: The form ì reproduces the historical transcription in the print. No Chinese character or modern pinyin equivalent is supplied here. (Trans.)

[^remusat-citation]: *Lettre sur le génie de la langue Chinoise à Monsieur Abel Remusat* [*Letter on the character of the Chinese language to Monsieur Abel Remusat*], p. 22. 98. (Humboldt)

[^editor-remusat-page]: [On Humboldt’s preceding citation:] Cf. above, p. 268. (1906 Ed.)

[^citation-number-context]: The unexplained terminal “98.” is retained from Humboldt’s note. The editor’s page 268 belongs to the 1906 German volume, rather than this English reading. (Trans.)
