## English translation

Here, however, one circumstance must not be overlooked. It does indeed lessen the disadvantage arising from the lack of genuine grammatical forms in languages, and could be regarded as a decisive objection to what has just been said. Even if a grammatical form has no designation in the language, it is nevertheless present, as a guiding law of understanding, in those who speak the language. At the same time, it is also indicated in the language in some way, however that may be. It may therefore even seem doubtful whether it should be attributed to the language or denied to it, since, after all, by language we understand not merely the dead sounds but also the speakers’ apprehension of them. Yet it remains no less necessary to distinguish this apprehension precisely from the dead sounds, and here to speak only of what actually lies in the sound perceptible to the senses. By grammatical form, therefore, I here understand an indication of the grammatical relation in the audible part of language itself that is so fixed and pure that the grammatical relation indicated is definitely recognized in it, and that the manner of indication cannot be taken as a designation of a concept at all but immediately, through itself, makes manifest the distinction between the designation of concepts and the designation of relations. Given the order deliberately chosen (134.), I can explain the manner of indication only later. Here I do not even exclude indication through word order, provided that it is absolutely fixed, immovable, and refers to a fixed point that is not itself recognizable merely by its position. If I now assume a case in which such an indication of a grammatical form as that just described is entirely absent from a language, or is effected not formally but through a concept with substantive content, the grammatical form is nevertheless brought into the language, and thus in turn also acts back from the language upon the mind. Thus, for example, in Chinese there is no designation of the verb as a grammatical form. But the language possesses words which, although grammatically they could also be nouns, are always taken only as verbs in the language. The Chinese speaker therefore never sees anything other than a verb in such a word, and for him the language consequently has verbs, provided one pays no attention to whether their verbal nature lies in a grammatical designation or in habitual linguistic usage. The same is found where a word in Chinese can be used equally well as a noun or as a verb, but the context of meaning or position indicates that the latter use is intended. Yet position in Chinese is not an indication of grammatical form in the sense just established, if only because it lacks a fixed centre outside itself. It also happens in languages that words originally possessing substantive content pass into actual relational words. In Chinese, ì is a verb and means to use, to employ. But it also serves as a preposition, and Mr Abel-Rémusat[^h1] very rightly observes that the nation, frequently using the word in this latter meaning, gradually forgets its original verbal sense entirely. In this instance, admittedly, such forgetting is more difficult, since the word is still current as a verb even today. But very often the situation is such that the original substantive meaning has disappeared entirely and can scarcely still be discerned etymologically; in that case, such a grammatical word functions solely as a relational concept. This, however, can only have happened little by little, through a gradual transition. Consequently, it is no longer always possible to tell from the language alone what is taken as truly grammatical, as a genuine and pure designation of relation; for that, one would have to know the nation’s manner of apprehension, and to know it at different epochs.

## Translator’s notes

- “Grammatical form” is explicitly restricted here to the fixed, pure audible indication described in the definition. The following examples concern Humboldt’s historical analysis of Chinese; the English does not certify that analysis as modern linguistic fact.
- “Substantive content” renders the related Sach- vocabulary in concept, word and meaning. It contrasts here with relation as such, and includes the action expressed by “use”; it does not mean “noun” or only a concrete object. Nor is it an automatic equation with a modern content-word class.
- In the sentence beginning “If I now assume,” “the grammatical form” resolves German sie. Its grammar also permits “the indication.” The following section’s statement that speakers put the concept of grammatical relations into language supports the chosen reading, without eliminating the syntactic ambiguity.
- “Mind” here renders Seele; “soul” remains an alternative. This local choice does not equate every Seele with Geist. “Apprehension” preserves Auffassung and its relation to Auffassungsweise, without treating understanding as merely hearing sound.
- The source has Latin Nomen/Nomina. “Noun” is the working English label here; its precise historical extension is not independently settled. “Used as a noun or as a verb” describes use, not a modern category-membership test.
- The historical transcription ì reproduces the coordinator’s reading of the accented italic i in print. No Chinese character, phonetic value or modern pinyin has been supplied. The footnote’s terminal 98 remains uninterpreted; the footnote’s original form is preserved in source.json.
- The final sentence retains the limit “not always,” “from the language alone,” and the need to know speakers’ manner of apprehension at different epochs. It does not say that etymology alone proves present grammatical function.
