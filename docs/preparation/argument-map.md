# Argument map of the complete printed work

This map follows GS V (1906), printed 364–473, §§1–149. It replaces the earlier contents-based outline as the continuous reading guide. Three readers covered every assigned OCR page, including examples and apparatus; their complete coverage logs are in [readings](readings/reading-early.md). This establishes an argument map, not a corrected transcription, expert validation of the linguistic examples, or a claim that every page has been visually collated. D001 still governs the reported cancellation of §§1–7.

## The American project and its method: 364–373, §§1–17

The opening justifies a continental inquiry through relative isolation and broad resemblance. It rejects uniform structure and radical difference from all other languages, while retaining explicit developmental rankings. Publication begins with a comparative account because complete grammars and dictionaries would delay it. Geography is a research frame, not a demonstrated language family.

The inquiry then distinguishes intrinsic description from historical/geographical connection. A list of grammatical devices cannot disclose a language's whole character: devices and their effects are different objects of comparison. Language is approached as a mental operation, shifting the natural-history analogy from anatomy toward physiology. Analysis of constituents remains necessary, but must lead to their coordinated treatment.

Study proceeds empirically through particulars; exposition may begin with the whole. The general *Typus* in §16 is a conceptual pattern of the mental process, abstracting individualizing circumstances. General grammar covers only part of that inquiry. It is not a proposed morphological class of languages. Anchors: EARLY-02–07, C01.

## Thought, sound and another speaker: 374–385, §§18–37

The announced outline includes language's effects on thought and feeling. Language forms thought; articulation and thought are analytically distinguished, then connected through bodily production and hearing. Thought becomes outwardly sensible and returns to the speaker as an object. The pleasure and abundance of speaking exceed practical cooperation.

Sound individuates a language, but the argument soon moves beyond sound. Sociability develops human powers; an I requires a Thou and an answering thinking power. Communication stimulates and tests thought, and understanding involves the hearer's own activity. Pronoun contrasts illustrate this relation; they do not establish a historical origin story.

Humanity, groups, nations, dialects and individuals form nested linguistic spheres. The proposal of human unity is expressly a personal conviction with an unexplained metaphysical basis. Acquisition is represented as growth of a power rather than mechanical word storage. Historical assertions about heredity and national faculties remain Humboldt's claims, not conclusions independently validated here. Anchors: EARLY-08–15.

## World-view, inheritance, likeness and individuality: 386–399, §§38–55

§39 connects the object's impression, subjective uptake and the word's contribution. A language-specific world-view emerges through their organized treatment; another language offers another standpoint. This is not yet a claim that every speaker shares one ideology.

§40 changes standpoint to language as inherited stock/system and relatively independent historical power. Dependence and independence interpenetrate: language must be regenerated in present thinking. Historical investigation begins amid inherited conditions, rather than at an observable pristine beginning.

Separation, contact and mixture yield gradual change. Shared elements do not establish one original language. Resemblance through historical connection differs conceptually from resemblance through common human capacities; §47 leaves their empirical separability open. The inquiry seeks distinctive character within likeness.

Individuality extends through the whole language (§49), then receives a further qualification: true individuality belongs to each speaker (§50). Understanding includes some non-understanding. Individual freedom works within inherited conditions and can produce unexpected uses. §55 recapitulates language as act and material, then divides words from discourse-connection as matter/form—expressly **not** sounds versus rules. Anchors: EARLY-16–22; C02–03.

## Sound before vocabulary and connection: 400–410, §§56–71

The sound system expresses a language's treatment of articulation. Physiological continua, national habits, sound distinctions and their alleged intellectual effects must remain separate claims. Here *Buchstabe* and *Alphabet* often concern spoken elements and their inventory, before writing is separately introduced.

Internal sound change can strengthen word unity; joining independent words can obscure divisions. Quantity, organizing accent and Chinese intonation are distinguished. Enclitic attachment complicates an elementary picture of separate words. Alphabetic writing makes transient sounds more distinct to reflection but can also encourage excessive systematization. These arguments include explicit aesthetic and developmental evaluations. Anchors: MIDDLE-01–07; C04.

## Word unity, formation and meaning: 410–433, §§72–105

A word unites sound and concept and becomes fully individual in thinking. A vocabulary list cannot disclose character without use and mutual restriction. Historical comparison requires internal sound and formation patterns; resemblance of sounds alone cannot establish descent. Roots, relative base words, derivation, composition and borrowing must be distinguished.

Persistent sound-forms can carry changing conceptual content. The abstract object of lexical study is not the whole living word. Logical, sensory and affective relations cooperate through imagination. Matter/form now has a word-level bearer, rather than automatically repeating §55's division.

Broad scope of one meaning differs from polysemy; dictionaries can exaggerate the latter by mixing times, genres and contexts. In §98 designation is considered conceptually, with the hearer's designation corresponding to the speaker's apprehension. Original motivation and motivation still felt are distinct.

§101 allows words to serve a sign's purpose but denies that this exhausts them: concepts receive completion through words. Symbol is a qualified comparison. Deleted footnotes must not be substituted for the active claim. Feeling can include an intellectual orientation rather than merely discrete emotions. Anchors: MIDDLE-08–16; C05–07.

## Vocabulary as a research object: 433–444, §§106–126

Vocabulary develops as an interconnected whole. Growth in sound-forms and growth in concepts have different trajectories; paraphrase can preserve communicative clarity while having different claimed intellectual effects. Apparent redundancy can support later distinctions.

An inventory of pure concepts independent of language cannot provide the desired neutral baseline. The practical substitute compares what languages actually express, using broad categories but acknowledging dependence on particular languages for individual concepts. Lexical and grammatical functions of roots differ. Forced derivation of every word is rejected; productive derivation belongs to vocabulary formation for this inquiry even when grammars conventionally describe it.

The final lexical standpoint examines feeling and historically changing apprehension. Objective and subjective analyses can concern different aspects of one phenomenon. Sound and broad meaning may seem unchanged while subjective force changes; literary records are needed to trace this. *Seele, Geist, Herz,* and *Gemüth* are examples, not a timeless faculty diagram. Anchors: MIDDLE-17–22; C08.

## Connected speech and its forms: 445–461, §§127–141

The starting point changes from given words to the expression of a whole thought. Expressions can be reused as units; analysis proceeds from speech into words. A constructional pattern should precede the account of individual parts. Philosophical concepts and historical facts are both indispensable: incomplete induction must not masquerade as necessity.

Lawfulness makes linguistic freedom possible. Logic and grammar overlap, but grammar adds existential positing, active predication and interpersonal address. Universal recoverability of relations does not entail identical grammatical views. §134 separates a language's ideal view of relations, technical means and actual sounds.

§136 distinguishes thought-form from language-form, rather than simply form from matter. Form is law, direction and procedure. Thought-form tends toward the *Satz*; language-form toward the *Periode*, which incorporates the conditions of intelligible linguistic expression. Their distinction needs an explanatory note rather than an automatic modern clause/sentence equation.

§139 distinguishes thought-laws, apprehension of the world and language's own form. The phrase *Anschauung der Welt* is not silently treated as an occurrence of *Weltansicht*. Imagination grasps productive speech-form through its possible arrangements, not exhaustive enumeration. Chinese receives both a contrasting evaluation and recognition of its systematic intellectual achievement. Anchors: LATE-01–14; C09–11.

## Grammar in the speaker and in language: 462–473, §§142–149

Grammar operating as a pattern/law in a speaker differs from grammar present in language. Tacit and explicit grammar restate the problem. Mental supplementation can supply relations without an independently overt grammatical expression; it need not mean conscious inference or an omitted spoken word.

Humboldt distinguishes designation of a substantive concept from marking a relation as relation. He attributes broader intellectual and expressive effects to genuine form while acknowledging the adequacy of alternative devices for understanding and many practical or scientific purposes. Unconditional linguistic domination of thought is rejected. These qualifications do not remove his evaluative hierarchy.

§147 isolates what audible language itself supplies. *Andeutung* can be a fixed, pure indication rather than a weak hint. Rigorously fixed word order is allowed in principle, subject to further conditions on 469–470; Chinese is said not to meet them. An expression's current relational function is not settled by its substantive origin alone.

§148's comparative questions are expressly denied the status of rigid classes on 472. §149 nevertheless upholds the distinction between intrinsic and supplied grammar. The work ends with an indefinitely graded boundary, not a sharply dividing taxonomy. Anchors: LATE-15–22; C12–14.
