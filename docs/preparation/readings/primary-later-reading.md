## Chinese paper: its own 1826 argument

**1. Grammar rather than writing, pp309–311.** The opening redirects attention from Chinese writing to the language's grammatical structure. Its proposed contrast concerns how thought-connections are indicated. The distinction between the meaning of a word and its grammatical classification is explicit: verbal conceptual content does not alone make a word a verb. The observer can assign familiar categories to every word without showing that those categories belong to the language in the relevant way.

The same discussion recognizes grammar expressed through inflection, grammatical words, and regular position, and grammar necessarily and determinately implied by sentence and discourse construction (p311). Therefore “not expressed by inflection” and “not grammatically present” cannot be treated as equivalent statements. The subsequent critique of Chinese asks whether its means mark the particular forms Humboldt expects, rather than denying meaningful relations or intelligible discourse.

**2. Thought form and linguistic contribution, pp312–316.** Logical judgement is compared with an equation, while inflected verbal predication supplies an imaginative linguistic presentation. The author differentiates cultivating conceptual relations directly from cultivating the language as an instrument with its own individually formed words. He acknowledges that obligatory grammatical determinations can restrict generality: Chinese can avoid specifying time or active/passive where the intended concept does not require it (p313).

The assertions that Chinese has no inflected verb, or no verb “als grammatische Form,” concern this stipulated grammatical bearer. They do not deny expressions of actions or verbal concepts. The analysis of alternate translations repeatedly distinguishes what the target translation specifies from what the Chinese expression itself determines. These are Humboldt's source-based analyses, not independently validated descriptions of Chinese in this preparation.

**3. Particles, position, and the work of understanding, pp317–320.** The account of `tchi` attributes separating and connecting effects to one expression, then explains its different translations through a common conceptual determination. On p318 `Begriffsbestimmung` is expressly contrasted with `Formenbezeichnung`. Position indicates which concept determines another, but cannot alone identify every required grammatical relation; meaning and discourse context remain necessary (p319).

The analysis of short clauses, implicit connections, and conventional expressions assigns work to the hearer. It does not establish that Chinese lacks structure because hearers infer connections. Conversely, it does not make every inferred connection an explicitly marked grammatical form. Preserve these three levels: conceptual relation, linguistic indication, and the hearer's reconstruction.

**4. Distinctive virtue and explicit hierarchy, pp321–324.** The paper praises Chinese regularity and consistency and differentiates it from the author's category of inadequately formed languages. It finds a distinctive intellectual pleasure in juxtaposed concepts. It also hypothesizes weaker activity of the relevant imagination in Chinese formation and explicitly ranks Chinese below the comparison languages overall (pp322–323).

These positions must remain visible together. The hypothesis about imagination, the proposed intellectual effects, and the overall ranking are not established modern results. The final pages tie their judgement to sentence structure and the range of grammatical means; the account is neither neutral taxonomy nor a simple condemnation. Thought and word are inseparable in one respect, yet a person can distinguish them and move beyond the limits of presently given language (p323).

## Lettre conclusion: evidential restraint and scope

Pages 305–306 distinguish inherited sound/word material, shaped by historical contacts, from intellectual organization and the later use of existing resources. The conclusion emphasizes that the philosophical character attributed to Chinese need not have arisen from new grammatical forms. It can be attributed to the use of existing means, much of the grammar being understood rather than overtly stated.

On p306 the author explicitly says his arguments have been based on ancient style. Pages 306–307 argue that modern style does not fundamentally alter the account; they also identify a limited evidential basis in Rémusat's examples and a small amount of copied and translated narrative. This extension should not be presented as an unrestricted investigation of spoken Chinese.

The dated closing asks for correction from deeper study and warns against reshaping general ideas around each newly analyzed language (pp307–308). That is a useful control on this preparation: apparent differences between texts must be recorded before deciding whether they express a common doctrine.

## Kawi introduction: a separately attributed later control

**1. Activity and historical material, pp45–48.** The active main text defines language as `Thätigkeit (Energeia)` rather than `Werk (Ergon)` and strictly locates this in each occasion of speaking. Language is the totality of such speaking; detached words and rules do not provide its full living reality. The discussion also insists that known languages stand in a historical middle and work on inherited material: activity reshapes what is given, rather than creating ex nihilo.

Page 47 gives its explicit definition of form as the constant and uniform in the work of making articulated sound express thought, grasped in connection and systematically represented. The scholarly abstraction is then distinguished from the living individual impulse it studies. Page 48 warns that complete isolated specification of this form cannot simply be extracted from a few distinctive features.

**2. Form, matter, and the evidence of particulars, pp49–53.** Linguistic form extends beyond grammatical form, productive word formation, and the conventional grammar/lexicon boundary. Matter is relative within a language: a root may be matter for declension but is already formed in another respect. The account does not place all physical sound on the matter side of one unchanging opposition.

The form of a language must be investigated through historical particulars and sound elements, not by collecting only its supposedly elevated or intellectual traits (p50). Borrowed Sanskrit words do not alone make Kawi a Sanskrit-related language in the stated genealogical sense (p51). On pp52–53 sound-form and its use in designation and thought-connection are distinguished as principles whose interpenetration constitutes individual linguistic form.

**3. Thought, sound, and understanding, pp53–59.** Language is presented as formative of thought; representation becomes concept through its relation to speech sound. Produced speech returns to the speaker's own ear, giving the representation objectivity while preserving its subjective origin (p55). “Objectivity” here names this relation, rather than automatically guaranteeing independently correct truth.

Understanding requires the hearer's activity: it is not transfer of a material content (p56). Articulation relates each word to a wider system of possible formations (p57). The account of acquisition resists mechanical accumulation of heard words (p58). Page 59 also advances assumptions about physical ancestry, national disposition, and linguistic affinity. Do not isolate the activity/acquisition passages as a contemporary theory while suppressing these adjacent historical assumptions.

## Consequences for the Grundzüge preparation

- Keep **form's bearer** explicit. A grammatical category, its audible indicator, a sentence's organization, and the Kawi definition of a language's formative method are distinct uses. A later umbrella definition does not retroactively fix every earlier occurrence.
- Keep **Bezeichnung** relational and local. In these pages it can concern thought-connections, a concept's determination, explicit grammatical marking, or an expression's role. Uniform “denotation” or “label” would lose several contrasts.
- Separate **word meaning, grammatical classification, and use**. The Chinese paper explicitly provides grounds for this distinction. It does not authorize equating Humboldt's classes with a modern grammatical framework.
- Use **speech/activity** passages as attributed comparisons. The Kawi Ergon/Energeia formulation and the broad definition of Form should not appear as quotations or implicit definitions of the Grundzüge unless independently established there.
- Preserve **history and epistemic limits**. The Lettre's stated basis, the Chinese paper's modal and evaluative claims, and the undated status of the supplied Kawi excerpt belong in any cross-text argument.
