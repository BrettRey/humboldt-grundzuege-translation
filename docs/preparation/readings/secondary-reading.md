## What the assigned sections contribute

- **Manchester, chapter 4, 67–79:** differentiates the universal conceptual task and relations of grammar from variable words, constructions, designation techniques, and sounds. His account of philosophical general grammar includes both overt designation and mentally supplied relations. It explicitly leaves some uncertainty about whether the universal relations are actually employed everywhere or merely available. This is a useful constraint on a simple opposition between universalism and diversity.
- **Manchester, chapter 4, 80–90:** offers an explicitly simplified arrangement of capacity, disposition, national character, language character, form, and inner form. His discussion does not finally reduce Form to either a dynamic force or a static system: the same item can form something while being the product of another forming activity. His report of the scarcity and late occurrence of innere Sprachform is a warning against making that term the master key to the earlier corpus, not a new corpus count verified in this task.
- **Manchester, chapter 6, 103–122:** reconstructs national character as an explanatory disposition, discusses its contested and speculative status, and follows the problem of causal priority from early essays through the later work. He distinguishes an inaccessible first origin from continuing reciprocal effects and inherited linguistic material. The later discussions give him grounds for stronger claims about national character as explanatory cause, but he does not establish a simple exclusively one-way relation. He acknowledges the difficulty of applying the proposed national psychology concretely.
- **Underhill, chapters 7–10, 53–80:** develops the Weltansicht/Weltanschauung distinction, emphasizes speech as living activity, explains form through recurring mental and linguistic work, and links concept-formation with cultivation. His account retains an external world while stressing the mediation of objects as understood. Historical reshaping of inherited language matters throughout; constant form is sustained through activity rather than being incompatible with it.
- **Underhill, glossary, 147–153:** separates several dimensions compressed by English worldview and identifies a number of terms as his own analytical devices. His explanation of subjectivation explicitly attributes that concept to Meschonnic. These are reception tools, not an unmarked list of Humboldt's own categories.
- **Trabant, chapter, 308–325:** presents a program of anthropological linguistics centered on diversity, creative cognition, reciprocal address, structural description, and the character revealed and developed in usage. The chapter distinguishes this program from a narrow history of sound laws, rigid typological classes, deterministic reception, and Chomskyan biological universalism, while acknowledging Humboldt's evaluative preference for inflection. Its reception history is relevant to avoiding later assimilations, but it is not direct evidence for a specific earlier textual reading.

## Terminological controls

### Language-form, inner form, and character

Use these as distinct questions before choosing English equivalents.

1. **What is doing the forming, and what is being formed?** Manchester's relational account of Form permits a grammatical system to act as form relative to its outputs and as formed material relative to a further principle (84–86/PDF 19–21). A forced choice between dynamic principle and static pattern loses that relation.
2. **What level of organization is at issue?** Manchester provisionally assigns inner form to syntactic and semantic organization, contrasted with phonetic organization (88–90/PDF 23–25). This is his reconstruction, not a warrant to add inner wherever Humboldt says Sprachform.
3. **Is character an explanatory orientation, an achieved specificity, or both?** Manchester's character operates as a partially determining disposition throughout structure; Trabant's inquiry into character depends on observing what speakers achieve with structures in texts and discourse. Neither makes character simply identical to a grammar inventory. Their different emphases must remain visible.

Underhill's account of durable but renewed patterns (75–79/PDF 88–92) does not contradict Manchester's refusal of an exclusively dynamic sense. Underhill explains how constancy is sustained; Manchester asks which item bears the form/matter relation at a given level. Both resist treating a grammar abstracted by the analyst as the whole of language.

### Weltansicht and Weltanschauung

Underhill, following Trabant, distinguishes linguistic world-presentation and concept-forming resources from an interpretation of the world as beliefs, ideology, or a more explicit outlook (55–57/PDF 68–70; 151–153/PDF 164–166). Opposed ideologies can inhabit one language and similar ideologies can travel across languages. This is a strong control against translating every worldview-related noun as a total doctrine.

The glossary itself describes this as a distinction inspired by Humboldt. It does not prove Humboldt maintained an invariant lexical opposition in every period and work. Apply it as a reason to inspect an occurrence, not as a replacement for inspecting it. Likewise, an occurrence of Ansicht, Anschauung, Welt, or their combination is not automatically an occurrence of a fixed technical term Weltansicht.

Underhill's world-perceiving, world-conceiving, cultural mindset, personal world, and perspective are explicitly his differentiating vocabulary. Subjectivation belongs to Meschonnic's development of the tradition. Mark these attributions whenever used in commentary.

### Development, formation, and cultivation

Underhill relates Bildung to individual cultivation, humanity's development, and the ongoing work of language (65–66/PDF 78–79). He also relates bilden to making concepts and organizes Bau, concept-formation, and cultivation as interacting processes (69–70/PDF 82–83). The relationship is substantial, but it is not lexical identity.

Consequently, distinguish at least:

- formation of a concept or expression in a particular act;
- establishment and recurrence of patterns over generations;
- historical reshaping of already inherited linguistic material;
- personal or collective cultivation, with an educational or normative dimension;
- evaluative progress toward a supposed more adequate linguistic organization.

The commentators' connected account does not justify rendering every development as cultivation, every formation as education, or every historical change as improvement. Underhill's warning that known languages reshape antecedent material (75–76/PDF 88–89) also blocks a gratuitous pristine-origin narrative. The OCR's Building on Underhill 65 appears corrupt; it is not adopted as a separate term.

### Sound and letters

Underhill locates speech in bodily utterance, audition, and reciprocal uptake; writing is a derivative and incomplete preservation requiring the reader to recover living speech (59–60/PDF 72–73; 69/PDF 82). Manchester treats phonetic organization as one aspect of form rather than as the whole linguistic organization (88–89/PDF 23–24).

This supports keeping audible sound, sound-organization, and writing separate. It does **not** supply historical philological evidence that every Humboldt Buchstabe is purely graphic, nor that every occurrence should be modernized to phoneme. The assigned secondary material contains no detailed Laut/Buchstabe analysis that resolves such cases. Preserve the local primary distinction and flag ambiguous terminology instead of importing a twentieth-century sound/letter boundary without evidence.

### Conceptual function and communication

All three accounts resist language as mere transport of independently complete thoughts. That does not make communication incidental. Underhill places the objectification and recurrence of concepts in speaking with others (69–70/PDF 82–83). Trabant makes the other person's understanding and return of the word integral to linguistic synthesis (312/PDF 5, image checked).

Accordingly, the contrast is between merely transmitting ready-made contents and an activity that forms thought through expression and reciprocal address. Do not manufacture an opposition in which cognitive language excludes interpersonal language. Conversely, Trabant's general account of I/you and Urtypus does not decide the referent of another Urtypus in *Grundzüge*.

### General grammar and mentally supplied grammar

Manchester separates necessary grammatical relations from their variable realization and from a particular language's ideal grammatical view (74–79/PDF 9–14). Explicit designation and mental supplementation are available within languages, even when whole languages exemplify contrasting tendencies. This blocks both the claim that missing morphology proves missing thought-relations and the claim that shared relations eliminate grammatical diversity.

His English conditional is a commentator's mood label; it is not independent evidence for which German grammatical term occurs in a particular primary passage. The same caution applies to his modern explanatory language of surface structure and universal grammar. Do not silently import their later technical senses.

## Differences of scope and interpretation

**Character is the clearest interpretive difference.** Manchester emphasizes a relatively simple national orientation that helps explain the phonetic, grammatical, and conceptual complexity of language (chapter 6, 117–121/PDF 16–20). Trabant emphasizes specificity attained and renewed in usage, especially literary, philosophical, and scholarly achievement, as the culminating object of inquiry (317/PDF 10, image checked). These accounts have a different center of explanation. They nevertheless agree that grammatical and lexical inventories do not exhaust character and that language and speakers influence one another. The excerpts do not justify inventing a clean contradiction between a wholly prior national essence and a wholly subsequent textual product.

**Universal relations and diversity are not rival hypotheses at the same level.** Manchester devotes sustained attention to conceptual universals. Trabant's closing criticism of Chomsky concerns the reduction of a historical and anthropological program to a biological core (324–325/PDF 17–18). His earlier treatment retains a universal task, shared human dispositions, and a common reality (312–313/PDF 5–6; 322–323/PDF 15–16). Thus their emphases differ without Manchester's universals refuting Trabant's diversity or Trabant's Chomsky criticism denying every universal commitment.

**Semantic-field reception is appraised differently.** Manchester finds syntactic-semantic organization a useful interpretation of inner form and regards semantic-field work as a development of that program (90/PDF 25). Trabant distinguishes productive structural inquiry from Weisgerber's deterministic and nationalist transformation (322–323/PDF 15–16). These evaluations concern different aspects and phases of a reception; neither passage establishes that every semantic-field analysis is deterministic.

**Historical ranking must remain explicit.** Trabant distinguishes recurring procedures from fixed language classes but also directly acknowledges the evaluative preference for inflection grounded in a philosophical account of synthesis (314–316/PDF 7–9; 315 image checked). He calls that ranking obsolete while explaining its coexistence with esteem for all languages and their resources. Avoid both retrospective endorsement and retrospective erasure. Denial of rigid classes does not entail denial of comparison or evaluative hierarchy.

## Operational limits for the translation register

- Record local senses rather than giving one secondary slogan authority over the entire term family.
- Give a primary occurrence its own page and context before using scholarship to explain it. A general statement about the Kavi introduction is not proof about an earlier fragment.
- Keep interpreter, primary author, and later development identifiable, especially for inner form, worldviews, national character, structuralism, and subjectivation.
- Preserve causal reciprocity and historical inheritance without making every claim symmetrical or causally indeterminate. Origins and continuing effects are separate questions.
- Retain Humboldt's evaluative vocabulary as his claim, with the qualifications actually present; do not silently replace it with current linguistic commitments.

No broader investigation is needed for these controls. Remaining local lexical decisions belong to the primary-passage comparison rather than further accumulation of secondary formulations.
