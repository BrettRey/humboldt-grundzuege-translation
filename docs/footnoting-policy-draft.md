# Footnoting policy — draft for discussion
<!-- SUMMARY: Proposed reader-facing annotation policy with examples from passages8–15 · status: draft, not adopted or applied · updated: 2026-11-16 -->

## Purpose and reader

Help an interested English reader follow Humboldt without knowing German or the history of linguistics. Give a reader grounds to question a consequential interpretation. Keep the English intelligible on its own wherever the source permits: improve an avoidably obscure translation before adding an explanation beneath it.

The guiding question is: **What would this reader misunderstand, miss or reasonably want to know at this point?** A note should answer an identifiable question, change the reader’s understanding or expose a consequential uncertainty.

## Three places for explanation

1. **Footnotes:** information needed at a particular passage—an important ambiguity, an unfamiliar reference or usage, a consequential historical qualification, or a textual problem affecting the reading.
2. **Introduction, glossary and longer commentary:** recurring terminology, the textual basis, the project’s translation principles and explanations that require sustained argument. Give a short local cross-reference when useful. Make the glossary available alongside standalone section exports.
3. **Linked scholarly apparatus:** all candidates, source alignments, term-rule associations, rejected alternatives, audit findings, decision histories and evidence limits. Preserve this material even when it does not warrant a printed footnote.

These layers share evidence and anchors. They serve different reading needs; a recorded translation choice does not automatically become a footnote.

## What deserves a footnote

- **Translation:** a plausible alternative changes the claim, scope, referent or important analogy. State the adopted reading, the alternative and the reason for the choice. Include the German only when it helps explain the issue.
- **Terminology:** an unfamiliar or historically specific use would otherwise mislead. Usually explain it at its first consequential occurrence and link later occurrences to the glossary. Discuss local exceptions where they matter; avoid suggesting that every German term has one invariant English equivalent.
- **Historical context:** identify a person, work, example or programme whose significance cannot reasonably be recovered from the English. The note about the fate of Humboldt’s projected grammars and dictionaries is a model of this function.
- **Textual status:** identify a variant, editorial intervention or reported cancellation where it bears on interpretation. Distinguish the printed reading from a report about the manuscript and from an independently checked manuscript fact. Explain recurring qualifications once in the appropriate introductory material, with local reminders when necessary.
- **Historical claims and evaluations:** add a focused correction or contextual explanation when leaving a claim unqualified would materially mislead. Preserve Humboldt’s assertion in the translation. Identify what he says and what the later evidence supports separately.

Routine stylistic choices, alternative English synonyms, model disagreements and ordinary changes of sentence structure normally stay in the apparatus. Readily understandable passages need no paraphrasing note. General explanations of our validation limits belong in the edition introduction rather than recurring beneath individual claims.

## Form, attribution and evidence

Use ordinary footnotes attached to the exact word, phrase or sentence concerned. Usually one to three sentences suffice; longer necessary discussions belong in commentary. Set no quota per page or section. Introduce useful distinctions without making readers process every discarded possibility. Keep one coherent issue per note, combining related explanations where that reduces repetition.

Write the reader’s answer first, followed by the necessary qualification and citation. Quote a German word or short phrase with an English explanation; do not require the reader to infer the point from untranslated German.

Distinguish **Humboldt’s own notes**, **the 1906 editor’s notes** and **translator’s notes** visibly. Translate Humboldt’s notes in full; explicitly label any summary of the 1906 apparatus. Preserve their separate authorship, source locators and original markers. Our commentary must not look like Humboldt’s wording or an editor’s independently verified finding.

Cite factual historical additions to checked evidence with useful page or section locators. Distinguish our reading of the German from claims made by a cited scholar. Neither model agreement nor uncorrected OCR is authority for a factual addition. If an important point remains unresolved, state the specific uncertainty briefly. An incidental unresolved detail can remain in the apparatus without prompting more research.

## Records and review

Give each note a stable identifier, separate from its displayed number. Attach it to the existing German and English sentence/span IDs; retain its text, authorship, citations, evidence and review status in the edition records. Keep superseded versions available. Check a note’s attachment when the translation changes; matching character positions alone do not establish that it still belongs there. Use the existing annotation and revision mechanisms; this policy proposes no new database architecture.

At each section checkpoint, check notes with the main text for accuracy, necessity, placement, duplication and accessibility. Source-check factual additions and verify that interpretive notes describe the actual alternatives fairly. Brett’s review assesses English clarity and usefulness; it does not certify German fidelity. Adoption of this policy would authorize its routine application within subsequent approved translation work, without a separate permission request for every note. Retrofitting existing sections would be a distinct, bounded application.
